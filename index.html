CHECK-MATE, the Automated Chess Board

A real chess board that plays you by itself. A sensor grid reads the positions and a CoreXY gantry carries an electromagnet beneath the board. H.G. Muller's micro-Max engine, adapted and retrofitted for the machine, runs natively on an Arduino with no computer in the loop.

Under each square is a Hall effect sensor detecting pieces, which passes what it sees to the Arduino, which then moves the opposing pieces using the CoreXY gantry.

Built to be played with. Difficulty is adjustable through search depth, so a beginner and a stronger player get a game rather than a formality. An LED matrix under the board lights the legal moves for a piece you pick up, and the moves the engine would consider best, which turns a game into a lesson. And every move is announced aloud, so someone who can't easily read a board across the table can still follow the game.

Provincial finalist, Hydro-Québec Science Fair. OIQ (Ordre des ingénieurs du Québec) award for technical complexity, OCTAS Youth Innovation award, and a Université de Sherbrooke scholarship.

Revisions

Three builds to get here

Across 18 months I prototyped three complete systems to reach a working board. Real geometry below, straight from the CAD.

MK1A purely conceptual design, built to work out base dimensions and find the main mechanical challenges. Working out the concepts and how to fit all that needs to be included on the inside of the board. Worked out a design language.
MK2Refined, with geometry that fits all the electronics, and an improved CoreXY system. Added housing for electronics. Added extra support for the top surface and designed it to be manufactured and assembled. Added space for extra cooling.
MK3Swapped from a reed switch base to Hall effect sensors to avoid floating switches, with a larger play area to allow better packaging, and nearer to a full-sized chess set. Hall effect sensors allowed for polarity readings instead of only basing itself on memory to track pieces. Integrated cooling, controls, LEDs, speakers and a liftable playing surface for maintenance.
What I had to work out System integration and electronics packaging. It was the first time I'd worked under so many constraints at once. Motors, drivers, and the electromagnet itself all threw electromagnetic interference across the sensor grid. Wiring, calibrating, and reading 64 individual sensors was its own problem, and came to over 40 m of wire. Shielding was added to all motor and sensor signal wires. Each sensor was calibrated with no interference, and with magnets. Electronics packaging was managed by adding components to all surfaces, including below the hinging playing surface.
Movement

Neither motor is an axis

CoreXY is a compact but more complex way to get cartesian movement, commonly used in 3D printers. Both motors stay bolted to the frame, so none of their weight moves. The trade is that neither one drives X or Y on its own, since every move is a combination of the two. Drag the magnet, or drive one motor at a time.

one motor alone gives a diagonal, which is the whole idea

Sensor grid

64 sensors, 8 pins

Under each square is a Hall effect sensor, which detects not only that a piece is there but also its polarity. Each one has three pins. Power and ground sit on shared rails, so only the signal leg is unique, and the 64 signals fan into four 16-channel multiplexers before reaching the Arduino. Hover a square to follow its path.

the select lines are shared, so all four mux read the same channel at once

The engine

Play the firmware

The chess itself is a minimax engine by H.G. Muller, retrofitted for my own chess board's needs: difficulty through search depth, an LED grid for legal moves, and an LED grid for best moves.

The algorithm calculates moves in a tree to see which future branch awards it the most advantage, while minimising the opponent's.

CRC Competition Robot

Over the course of 8 months, I developed the robot for John Abbott's robotics team, competing in the Canadian Robotics Competition. I built a swerve drive drivetrain, and a dual claw mechanism to both stack and move game pieces, movable in all three axes independently from the drive base.

These ideas placed us 2nd for Robot Design.

Subsystems

Drivetrain – Swerve Drive

A swerve drive is a powered wheel that can steer in all 360 degrees. Syncing the steering and drive of all four wheels lets the robot drive in any direction without changing its orientation.

Designed to be swapped, not repaired. Each module is self-contained and held in by just four bolts. Take them out and the whole module drops out from below, and a fresh one goes in the same way.

  • 30 sto exchange a module
  • 4bolts, the only tools needed
Claw

Two chopsticks, one game piece

The claw doesn't grip. Two arms on fixed pivots act like chopsticks: each one stays tangent to the game piece, one on either side, so swinging them pushes the piece side to side while it's held from both directions the whole way. The game scores a multiplier for stacked pieces, so placement has to be exact. The chopstick motion adds extra movement in the Y direction on its own, lining a piece up over the stack while the base stays still. Drag the piece, or grab either arm.

at either end of its travel, the near arm points straight down

Teaching a Wheel-Legged Robot to Balance

A two-wheeled robot with legs, and the neural network that drives it. Nobody wrote its balance controller. It learned one in simulation, by trial and error, using reinforcement learning (PPO): about 25 million control steps, or 69 hours of simulated driving.

Below is the trained policy driving in the MuJoCo simulator. The network beside it is the real one: its 22,406 weights are running in your browser, on the same sensor readings the robot had, deciding what each motor does next. The ⚡ clips are a second policy, retrained with the hardware limits taken off — the last page explains what that means.

The network

What it sees, and what it moves

Every 10 ms the policy gets 39 numbers and returns 6. There is no memory and no physics model inside it: two layers of 128 neurons, each squashing its input through tanh, map the robot's state straight to motor commands.

39 inputs

  • Which way is down, in the robot's frame3
  • How fast it's rotating (gyro)3
  • Hip angles and speeds8
  • Wheel speeds2
  • Its own previous output6
  • The command: speed, turn rate, height error3
  • Motor winding temperatures6
  • Wheel odometry and accumulated tracking error3
  • Accelerometer3
  • Ride height, commanded and actual2

6 outputs

  • Hip position targets, front and rear, each side4
  • Wheel torques2

The hips are position-controlled: a PD loop at 1 kHz turns each target into torque, through a 12:1 printed planetary gearbox. The wheels get torque directly. Balancing is the wheels' job; the legs set ride height and soak up the ground.

Inputs are normalised with statistics collected during training, and those statistics are part of the policy. Export the weights without them and the network still passes its own verification, then drives nonsense. That exact bug slipped through once, so the exporter now refuses to run without them.

The same weights are exported to a C header for the robot's microcontroller, and the C forward pass is checked against the Python network: they agree to within 4×10−7.

Learning

Scored, not programmed

PPO learns by doing. Six copies of the simulator run side by side. The policy drives each one, every step earns a score, and the network is nudged toward whatever scored better than expected. The nudges are small and clipped, so one lucky run can't wreck what it already knows.

Earns, every step

  • Driving at the commanded speed1.5
  • Staying upright0.8
  • Turning at the commanded rate0.6
  • Holding ride height0.5
  • Still being alive0.5

Pays for

  • Jerky or saturated motor commands
  • Leg torque and joint speed
  • Heating the motors past what they can shed
  • Sliding sideways, hitting the ground hard
  • Falling over: −5, and the episode ends

Difficulty follows a curriculum. Training starts on flat ground with a perfect robot that is mostly asked to stand still. Over the first 40% of training the ground gets rougher, the shoves harder and the robot's physical properties more random, until it is balancing a robot it can't quite know.

What I had to work out Reading why episodes ended, not just how long they lasted. Mid-run, episode length fell from 1860 to 1160 steps, which is exactly the dip a harder curriculum causes. It was a bug. The crouch command had been derived from nominal motor values, and as the randomisation widened, 92.5% of the simulated robots were being asked to hold a pose they physically could not. Another bug read the velocity reward in the world frame instead of the robot's, so it scored the wrong axis whenever the robot turned. Forward tracking was stuck at 52% of the command until I found it.
Sim to real

A simulator that's worse on purpose

A policy that only works on a perfect simulated robot is useless on a real one. So the simulator is deliberately harsher than reality. The motors are modelled from their electrical constants, with a back-EMF torque ceiling and a 1 A current limit. The windings heat up. Every episode draws a different robot.

Randomised every episode

  • mass ±15% + 0–150 g payload
  • centre of mass
  • floor friction 0.25–1.4
  • gearbox efficiency 55–92%
  • backlash 0–3°
  • 0–40 ms latency, dropped frames
  • IMU mounting error, gyro bias
  • encoder offset and noise
  • motor supply 8.0–9.6 V
  • tyre radius ±4%
  • 12 mm bumps, 18 mm steps
  • shoves up to 0.7 m/s
  • 1 kHzphysics, MuJoCo
  • 12:1printed planetary hips
  • 62208 gimbal motors
  • 170 mmtrack width
Where it stands It isn't finished, and the demo doesn't hide that. At nominal conditions it balances, drives, spins on the spot and recovers from shoves. Turn rate tracks within a few percent, but forward speed undershoots, reaching about two-thirds of a fast command (the speed gauge under the demo shows the gap). It also ignores ride-height commands. Under full randomisation it still fails about half of its 20-second episodes, and it can't yet reliably drive off an 18 mm step. Next comes validation, then the real robot.
Demo mode

The same robot, with the limits taken off

Everything above is bound by what my hardware can actually do. This isn't. I took the trained policy and kept training it in a simulator with the physical limits removed, to see what the method does when the motors stop being the constraint. It is the ⚡ clips in the player, and it will never run on the real robot.

What I took off

  • Motor supply, 9.4 V16.8 V
  • Current limit, 1 A3 A
  • Wheel torque, 0.05 N·m0.15
  • Winding heat modeloff
  • Every randomised propertyoff

What I asked for instead

  • Speed, ±1.2 m/s±2.8
  • Turn rate, ±2.5 rad/s±6.0
  • Shoves, 0.7 m/s1.2
  • Bumps and steps, 12 / 18 mm16 / 24

Eight million more steps, three and a half hours. It turns at 5.8 rad/s of a commanded 6.0, takes 1 m/s shoves standing up, and drives over the 24 mm step edges the honest policy fell off every single time. Survival went from about half of its episodes to 81%.

What it doesn't fix Torque was never the whole problem. It still undershoots forward speed, reaching 1.24 m/s of a commanded 1.8, and it still ignores ride-height commands entirely — both of which the honest policy also does, so neither is a power limit. A shove landing while it drives and turns at the same time still puts it on its side, on five of six starts. The interesting part of the demo is which failures survived having their cause removed.
